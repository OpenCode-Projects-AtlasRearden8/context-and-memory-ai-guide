Read Me First: The Two-Minute Orientation

Context and Memory: The AI Guide Nobody Passed to You

Hey. Before you dive into the chapters, here's the whole book in two minutes. If you only remember these five things, you'll be ahead of most people who use AI every day.

1. AI reads in "tokens," not words

When you type "the," that's roughly one token. When you type "unbelievable," that's two or three. Tokens are how AI counts its own attention and your text — and once you see it, a lot of odd AI behavior suddenly makes sense. Chapter 1 covers it.

2. Every chat has a container, and it fills up

The context window is the AI's working memory for one chat — everything you've said, everything the AI has said, plus any documents you pasted. It's big, but it is not bottomless. Long threads quietly push older stuff toward the edge. Chapter 2.

3. Separate chats mostly don't know about each other

Start a new chat, and you're usually talking to an AI with amnesia of everything important from the last one. Some tools remember things about you across chats (Chapter 8), but your project details, decisions, and context don't follow you. Chapter 3.

4. Old chats quietly degrade

The longer a thread runs, the more likely the AI is to: forget your formatting rules, mix up your project with something you mentioned twenty messages ago, or drift toward generic answers. This isn't the AI being dumb — it's a full container plus a pile of old text pulling attention around. Chapters 7 and 4.

5. The pros start fresh chats on purpose

This is the skill. Start a fresh chat, paste a tidy summary of the context that matters, and you get noticeably better answers than you would by keeping one 400-message mega-thread. Chapter 5 gives you a copy-paste "context header" for exactly this.

How this manual works

  • Plain English first. Terms like "context window" get defined in real words before anything technical.
  • Every chapter ends with "Try this" — a small, low-effort experiment.
  • Three extras at the end: a worksheet to audit your own habits, a checklist for when things feel off, and a glossary you can actually skim.

A note on safety

This guide covers AI tool layouts and behaviors as described by their makers and observed in use. Product interfaces change constantly — menus get renamed, settings move. When a chapter says "the setting is usually under…," treat it as a map, not a photograph. The concepts transfer even when the buttons change.

And the usual: this book is educational, written by a nurse-turned-AI-author, not a lawyer or your doctor. It never touches medical, legal, or financial advice — though the thinking skills here will transfer to any of those conversations.
